\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

Automated spike sorting has become indispensable for modern electrophysiology, yet the quality-control step remains difficult because the most meaningful error targets require ground truth. This thesis studies whether two spike-sorting error metrics, \fpos{} and \fmiss{}, can be predicted for real paired recordings by transferring supervision from much larger SpikeForest hybrid datasets. The central challenge is not model capacity alone, but domain shift: hybrid data are abundant and labelled, whereas paired recordings are scarce, biologically closer to deployment, and structurally heterogeneous.

The work builds a leakage-safe benchmark that separates recordings rather than units, preventing recording-level context from leaking into evaluation. The final packaged dataset contains 33,206 rows from 61 recordings and 9 spike sorters. Of these rows, 24,224 come from hybrid data and 8,982 from paired data, while only 207 paired rows carry the matched labels used for the target benchmark.

Three findings define the manuscript. First, the hybrid-to-paired shift is severe: domain-classifier AUC reaches 0.9765 with unit-only features and 0.9997 with full recording context, showing that the transfer problem is structurally hard rather than cosmetically different. Second, the transfer story is sharply asymmetric. For \fpos{}, context-aware transfer models achieve strong performance, with the strongest robust structured stack reaching 0.4068 mean \rsq{} across 20 recording-disjoint seeds, while the paired-only raw baseline reaches 0.4209. For \fmiss{}, the picture is much weaker: the best retained 20-seed mean \rsq{} is only -0.0128, and unseen-family performance remains clearly negative overall. Third, the preferred \fpos{} recipe depends on evaluation regime and held-out family. Under recording-disjoint evaluation, the pre-waveform unlabeled-structure stack is the main robust transfer reference, whereas under leave-one-family-out evaluation the waveform-augmented structure model becomes slightly stronger overall and wins 3 of the 5 held-out families.

The main scientific conclusion is therefore not that one universal model is best. Instead, leakage-safe quality prediction is feasible for \fpos{}, much less stable for \fmiss{}, and strongly shaped by domain shift, label scarcity, biological regime, and family heterogeneity. The strongest contribution of the thesis is a benchmark-oriented account of when automated spike-sorting quality prediction works, when it fails, and why.
